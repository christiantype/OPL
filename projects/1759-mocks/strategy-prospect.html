The Pitch

Gen-Skip Split-Dollar, also known as an intergenerational split-dollar arrangement (I-SDA), is an advanced legacy planning strategy that preserves illiquid family legacy assets (such as family businesses, real estate, and private equity) for descendants with unmatched tax-efficiency.

The plan involves a senior family member (parent or grandparent) funding an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) to purchase insurance on the life of a junior family member (child or grandchild). At the junior family member's passing, a cash death benefit is paid to the ILIT free of income tax and wealth transfer tax, providing the liquidity needed to maintain legacy assets for future generations.

When it fits

The Prospect Curator surfaces this strategy when a client shows significant illiquid assets — real estate, a closely-held business, or private equity — a low remaining exemption, and a desire to keep those assets in the family rather than force a sale to cover transfer taxes.

It's strongest for families who are asset-rich but liquidity-poor, where a forced sale at death would mean selling the very thing the legacy is built on.

How it works

1 · Fund the ILIT. A senior family member funds an irrevocable life insurance trust through an economic-benefit split-dollar arrangement, keeping gift-tax exposure minimal.

2 · Insure the junior life. The ILIT buys insurance on a younger family member, so the death benefit compounds across a longer horizon.

3 · Receive the benefit. At the junior's passing, a cash death benefit lands in the ILIT — income-tax-free and outside the taxable estate — providing the liquidity to keep the legacy assets intact.

Objections, answered

"Won't the IRS challenge the valuation?" Post-Levine, an arrangement structured as economic benefit — not a loan — has held up, with the receivable discount the court accepted as the anchor.

"What if the client is uninsurable?" Insure a younger, healthier life; the benefit still routes through the trust to the same beneficiaries.

"Isn't this aggressive?" No — every figure and assumption is shown, sourced, and walkable in front of a client. The math is transparent, not hidden.

Related court cases

Estate of Levine v. Commissioner — the taxpayer win that anchors the economic-benefit approach.

Estate of Cahill v. Commissioner — the cautionary tale on retained control; structure matters.

Estate of Morrissette v. Commissioner — early validation of the intergenerational split-dollar arrangement.

The numbers, in depth

Start with a $30M illiquid estate growing at 5%. Without planning, estate and transfer taxes take roughly 40% off the top — and because the assets can't easily be sold, heirs are often forced to liquidate a business or property just to cover the bill.

Funded correctly, the death benefit lands in the trust income-tax-free and outside the estate, replacing what taxes would have taken. The receivable's valuation discount — the figure courts have accepted — is what makes the gift-tax math work, and it's the number an advisor should always pull forward.

Risks to flag

Retained control. Keep the senior family member out of the trust's controls — Cahill is the reminder of what happens otherwise.

Premium financing. If premiums are financed rather than gifted, the gift-tax analysis changes; model both.

Health & timing. The plan leans on insuring a younger life — confirm insurability early so the structure doesn't stall at underwriting.

Questions clients ask

"Is this a loophole?" No — it's a recognized structure with decades of case law behind it. Everything is disclosed and valued.

"What if the law changes?" The trust is irrevocable but the plan is reviewed annually; the AI flags rulings that touch your open cases.

"Who actually owns the policy?" The trust does — which is the whole point. It keeps the proceeds out of the taxable estate.

How to position it

Lead with the problem the client already feels: "If something happened tomorrow, your family would have to sell the thing you spent a lifetime building, just to pay the tax."

Then show the gap — the two after-tax numbers, side by side — and let the difference do the talking. Walk the diagram once, slowly. The goal isn't to dazzle with structure; it's for the client to understand exactly how their legacy stays intact.
